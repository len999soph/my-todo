## Setting the scene

In April 1865, the Civil War ended. The North (the Union) had won, and the country
was technically whole again. But "the war is over" did not mean "everything is fine."
Imagine a giant group project that completely fell apart — people fought, things got
broken, and now everyone has to figure out how to work together again. That's the
challenge the United States faced. We call this rebuilding period **Reconstruction**.

There were two huge problems to solve:

1. **How do you bring the Southern states back into the United States?** They had
   tried to leave and form their own country (the Confederacy). What were the rules
   for letting them back in?
2. **What happens to the roughly 4 million people who had just been freed from
   slavery?** Were they citizens? Could they vote? Could they own land, go to school,
   or get paid for work? Almost nothing was decided yet.

How the country answered these questions would shape America for the next 100 years.

## The South after the war

The South was wrecked. Much of the fighting had happened there, so farms, railroads,
and whole cities (like Atlanta) were burned or destroyed. The Southern economy had
been built on enslaved labor growing cotton — and now that system was gone. Many
white Southerners were angry and bitter about losing the war. Meanwhile, formerly
enslaved people were free for the first time, full of hope, but with no money, no
land, and no easy way to make a living.

## Three amendments that changed everything

The biggest, most lasting results of Reconstruction were three additions to the
Constitution. They're called the **"Reconstruction Amendments,"** and you should
know them cold:

- **13th Amendment (1865):** Ended slavery everywhere in the United States. This is
  the one that made freedom permanent and national.
- **14th Amendment (1868):** Said that anyone born in the US is a **citizen** — and
  that states must give all citizens **"equal protection of the laws."** This is one
  of the most important amendments in all of US history; it still gets used in court
  today.
- **15th Amendment (1870):** Said a citizen cannot be denied the right to **vote**
  because of race. In practice, this meant Black men gained the right to vote.
  (Notice: women of all races still could not vote — that fight came later.)

A memory trick: **13 – free, 14 – citizen, 15 – vote.** Freedom, then citizenship,
then a voice. Each one builds on the last.

## Help, and hope

To help millions of people start new lives, the government created the
**Freedmen's Bureau**. It set up schools (many freed people had been denied the
chance to learn to read), helped people find work, and provided food and medical
care. For a brief time, Black men were elected to office — including to the US
Congress — and Black communities built churches and schools that lasted for
generations.

## How most freed people ended up working: sharecropping

Freed people wanted **land** of their own, but most never got any. So a system called
**sharecropping** developed. A landowner let a family farm a piece of land, and in
return the family gave the owner a large *share* of the crop they grew. It sounds
fair, but it usually wasn't: prices and debts were set against the farmer, so
sharecroppers often ended up trapped in debt, unable to leave or get ahead. It tied
many Black (and some poor white) families to land they would never own.

## The backlash, and the end of Reconstruction

Many white Southerners resisted these changes — sometimes violently. Hate groups like
the **Ku Klux Klan** used terror to stop Black people from voting and to scare them
out of using their new rights.

Then, in **1877**, Reconstruction ended. As part of a political deal, the federal
government pulled its troops out of the South. Without that protection, Southern
states quickly passed laws to take rights back away from Black citizens. These were
the **Jim Crow laws** — rules that forced **segregation** (separating people by race)
in schools, trains, restaurants, and almost everywhere else. Tricks like poll taxes
and literacy tests were used to stop Black men from voting, even though the 15th
Amendment said they could.

## So… did Reconstruction succeed or fail?

This is exactly the kind of "big thinking" question your teachers love, and there's
no single right answer — which is what makes it a great essay question.

- **In some ways it failed:** Within a few years, segregation and unfair laws erased
  much of the progress. Real equality would not come for almost another 100 years,
  with the Civil Rights Movement of the 1950s and 60s (Unit 8.9!).
- **In some ways it succeeded:** The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments never went away.
  They stayed in the Constitution and became the legal *foundation* that the Civil
  Rights Movement later used to win. Reconstruction planted seeds that bloomed much
  later.

**The connection to remember:** Reconstruction is why the Civil Rights Movement was
even possible. The promise made in the 1860s is the promise people demanded the
country keep in the 1960s.
